4. Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age)

The Ice Age ends — tiny "microlith" tools, bows and arrows, the first tamed animals.

Tell me more

🌦️ What changed?

The world warmed up. More grass and forests grew, with smaller, faster animals like deer. So people made microliths — stone blades smaller than your fingernail — and fixed them into wood or bone to make arrows, sickles and harpoons.

🐐 First tamed animals

At Bagor (Rajasthan) and Adamgarh (MP), we find some of India's earliest signs of people keeping sheep and goats instead of only hunting them.

🛕 What did they believe?

People were buried with care, with ornaments and tools — at Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama (Pratapgarh, UP). Bhimbetka paintings now show dancing, honey-collecting and family scenes.
🧵 Thread to today: Bows and arrows and wild-honey gathering are still part of life for some forest communities in India.

5. Neolithic (New Stone Age)

Farming! People settle down in villages, make pottery and polished stone axes.

Tell me more

🌱 The big change

At Mehrgarh (Balochistan, now Pakistan) people grew wheat and barley, kept goats, sheep and cattle, built mud-brick houses, and later grew cotton. They also made polished stone axes, grinding stones and pottery.

🗺️ Farming spreads — at different times

Burzahom (Kashmir): pit-houses dug into the ground for winter, and dogs buried with their owners! Chirand (Bihar): bone tools. Lahuradewa & Koldihwa (UP): early rice. Utnur, Sanganakallu, Budihal (Karnataka/AP): cattle-herders whose dung heaps became "ash mounds". Daojali Hading (Assam): axes linked to Southeast Asia.

🦷 Wow — ancient dentists!

At Mehrgarh, archaeologists found teeth with tiny holes drilled into them about 9,000 years ago — some of the earliest dentistry known anywhere in the world.
🧵 Thread to today: Wheat, barley, rice, cattle, clay pots, mud houses and cotton thread — all born or grown here, all still part of daily Indian life.

6. Chalcolithic (Copper–Stone Age)

The first metal! Copper tools join stone ones in painted-pottery villages.

Tell me more

🔬 What's new?

"Chalco" = copper, "lithic" = stone. People learned to melt copper from rocks and beat it into axes, bangles and fish-hooks — but still used plenty of stone tools. Their pots were often painted black-on-red.

🗺️ Which cultures?

Ahar–Banas (Rajasthan — copper from the Aravalli hills), Kayatha and Malwa (MP, e.g. Navdatoli on the Narmada), Jorwe (Maharashtra — Inamgaon, Daimabad). In the Ganga–Yamuna region: the Copper Hoard people, with copper harpoons and strange human-shaped copper pieces.

👨‍👩‍👧 How did they live?

Big farming villages. Inamgaon had a chief's house, grain stores and a water channel for irrigation. At Daimabad, a hoard of bronze figures was found — a chariot with a rider, an elephant, a rhino and a buffalo!
⏳ Overlap alert: Many of these village cultures lived at the same time as — and even after — the Harappan cities. India never moved in one single step!
🏘️
c. 3300 – 2600 BCE

7. Early Harappan (villages become towns)

Along the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra rivers, villages grow into walled towns.

Tell me more

👨‍👩‍👧 How did they live?

Bigger settlements with town walls, mud-brick houses and pottery made on a potter's wheel. Marks scratched on pots may be the first steps towards a script.

🗺️ Where?

Kot Diji, Amri, Rehman Dheri (Pakistan), the early "Ravi phase" at Harappa, and early layers at Kalibangan (Rajasthan), Bhirrana and Rakhigarhi (Haryana).

🌾 Wow — the oldest ploughed field!

At Kalibangan, archaeologists found a field with criss-cross plough furrows — one of the oldest ploughed fields in the world. Two crops were probably grown in the same field.
🧵 Thread to today: Farmers in Rajasthan still plough criss-cross to sow mustard and gram together in one field.
🏙️
c. 2600 – 1900 BCE

8. Mature Harappan (the great cities)

Planned cities with drains, a Great Bath, seals, standard weights and trade across the sea.

Tell me more

🏙️ The cities

Mohenjo-daro, Harappa (Pakistan), Dholavira (Gujarat — clever water reservoirs and a giant signboard), Lothal (a dockyard for boats), Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi, Banawali, and Chanhudaro (a bead factory). It covered a bigger area than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia put together!

🔬 What did they invent?

Streets in a grid, covered drains, baked bricks all in the same 1:2:4 shape, the Great Bath, and stone weights that doubled — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32… Their cotton cloth is among the oldest in the world.

📚 What did they write?

Small square seals carved with animals (like the "unicorn") and about 400 signs. Nobody has been able to read the Harappan script yet — maybe you will!

🌏 Who did they meet?

Traders sailed to Mesopotamia (Iraq), whose clay tablets mention a land called "Meluhha" — very likely the Harappans. They sold carnelian beads, cotton and timber.

🎨 What did they create?

The bronze "Dancing Girl" (made by the lost-wax method), the stone "Priest-King", toy carts and whistles of baked clay.

9. Late Harappan (the cities fade)

Big cities slowly empty out, and people spread east and south into smaller villages.

Tell me more

❓ Why did the cities fade?

Probably many reasons together: weaker monsoons, rivers changing course, the Ghaggar-Hakra river (often linked with the Sarasvati) drying up, floods, and trade with Mesopotamia falling off. It was not one sudden invasion — historians have dropped that old idea.

👨‍👩‍👧 How did life change?

Writing, seals and standard weights slowly disappear. But farming village life carries on — in cultures like Cemetery H (Punjab), Jhukar (Sindh) and Rangpur & Rojdi (Gujarat), with more sites now in Haryana and western UP.

🛞 Wow — Sinauli!

At Sinauli (UP), around 2000–1800 BCE, people were buried with wooden carts covered in copper sheets, along with swords and shields.
🧵 Thread to today: Bangles, bullock carts, ploughs, cotton cloth, clay toys and the swastika symbol all carried on long after the cities were gone.

10. Early Vedic (the Rigvedic age)

Cattle-herding tribes in the "land of seven rivers" compose the hymns of the Rigveda.

Tell me more

🗺️ Where?

Sapta Sindhu — "the seven rivers": the Indus and its Punjab rivers (Vitasta/Jhelum, Asikni/Chenab, Parushni/Ravi, Vipas/Beas, Shutudri/Sutlej) and the Sarasvati. The Ganga is mentioned only once!

👨‍👩‍👧 Who were they?

They spoke an early form of Sanskrit, a cousin of Persian, Greek and Latin (pitar → pater → father!). How this language reached India is still debated: many scholars think speakers came in slowly from Central Asia over centuries and mixed with people already here; others argue it grew within India. New DNA studies (like one from Rakhigarhi) are adding fresh clues.

🐄 How did they live?

Mostly herders — cattle were wealth, and the word for battle, gavishti, means "searching for cows"! They grew barley, used horses and chariots, and lived in houses of wood and reed.

👑 Who ruled?

Each tribe (jana) had a chief called the rajan, guided by assemblies — the sabha, samiti and vidatha — where women could also take part. People gave the chief gifts called bali.

🛕 What did they believe?

Gods of nature: Indra (rain and war), Agni (fire, the messenger to the gods), Varuna (guardian of rita, the order of the universe), Ushas (dawn), Surya, Soma. Worship was the fire ritual — yajna. No temples, no idols.

📚 What did they compose?

The Rigveda — 1,028 hymns in 10 books — passed on by memory, not writing, for about 3,000 years, word for word. Women such as Lopamudra, Ghosha and Apala composed hymns too.
⚔️ The Battle of Ten Kings: On the banks of the Parushni (Ravi), King Sudas of the Bharata tribe defeated ten tribes. The name "Bharat" for our country traces back partly to this tribe!
🧵 Thread to today: The Gayatri mantra comes from the Rigveda. Walking around Agni at weddings, havans, and Vedic chanting (honoured by UNESCO) are all still alive.
⚔️
c. 1000 – 600 BCE

11. Later Vedic (iron, kingdoms & big questions)

People move into the Ganga plains with iron tools; tribes become kingdoms; the Upanishads ask "Who am I?"

Tell me more

🗺️ Where?

Eastwards into the Ganga–Yamuna plains. The Kuru and Panchala kingdoms (around Delhi and western UP) were the most important; later Kosala, Kashi and Videha rose further east. Towns like Hastinapura appear.

🔬 What did they invent?

Iron — called shyama ayas, "the dark metal". Iron axes cleared thick forests and iron ploughs broke harder soil. Their special pottery: Painted Grey Ware (grey with black designs). The Shulba Sutras used clever geometry to build fire altars — including the rule we now call Pythagoras' theorem!

👨‍👩‍👧 How did they live?

Now settled farmers — rice, wheat, barley, lentils. Society became stricter: varna (four groups) started to depend on birth, gotras appeared, and the idea of life-stages (ashramas) began. Women lost their place in assemblies, though thinkers like Gargi and Maitreyi still debated with sages.

👑 Who ruled?

Tribes became territories — jana became janapada ("where the tribe puts its foot"). Kings grew powerful, collected regular taxes, and held grand rituals: Rajasuya (crowning), Ashvamedha (the horse sacrifice) and Vajapeya (a chariot race).

📚 What did they write?

The other three Vedas — Samaveda (melodies — roots of Indian music), Yajurveda (ritual verses), Atharvaveda (charms, healing, everyday life) — then the Brahmanas, Aranyakas ("forest books") and the Upanishads.

🛕 What did they believe?

Rituals became huge and costly — but the Upanishads asked deeper questions: What is the self (atman)? What is the universe (brahman)? What happens after death? Ideas of karma and rebirth take shape.
🌏 Meanwhile in the South: From about 1200 BCE, people in South India began using iron too, and buried their dead under great stone circles called megaliths — at Brahmagiri (Karnataka) and Adichanallur (Tamil Nadu).
🧵 Thread to today: India's national motto "Satyameva Jayate" (Truth alone triumphs) comes from the Mundaka Upanishad. The stories of the Mahabharata (Kuru kingdom) and Ramayana (Kosala, Videha) are set in this world, though they were written down much later.
🧵 Tantu says: By about 600 BCE, the janapadas have grown into 16 great kingdoms — the Mahajanapadas — and two young princes named Siddhartha and Vardhamana are about to ask questions that will change the world. That's where my thread goes next!
