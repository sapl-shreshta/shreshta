The First Artists

Upper Palaeolithic · the late Old Stone Age · c. 40,000 – 12,000 years ago

🧵

Namaste! Tantu here, and this stop is special. The people we meet now are exactly like you — same brains, same bodies, same ability to talk, laugh, sing and imagine. If one of them were born today, they could go to your school! And they start doing something no one before them did: making things just because they're beautiful.

ACT ONE · BEADS BY THE FIRE

About 25,000 Years Ago, at Patne in Today's Maharashtra

It's the Ice Age. The air is cooler and drier than India today, and wide grasslands stretch across the Deccan. Out on the grass, a flock of ostriches — yes, ostriches, in India! — stalks along, keeping a wary eye on the hunters.

Evening falls. A family sits around a fire beside a river. A girl holds a piece of ostrich eggshell — thick, creamy-white and strong. With a tiny stone drill, she twists and twists until a neat hole goes through. Then she rubs the piece on a flat stone until it's round and smooth. A bead. It's her ninth today.

Her grandmother is scratching a pattern onto a bigger piece of shell — a neat criss-cross of lines. Nobody needs that pattern to hunt or to eat. She makes it because she wants to. Because it means something to her. That's something new in our story.

🔥📿🥚
They were modern humans — Homo sapiens, our own species. Their ancestors had walked out of Africa and spread along the coasts and river valleys of Asia. Exactly when they first reached India is still debated — somewhere between about 50,000 and 80,000 years ago — but by this time they were living all over the subcontinent. Most people in India today have these first settlers among their ancestors.
So much of the world's water was frozen into giant ice sheets that the sea was much lower — around 100 metres lower at the coldest time! Sri Lanka was joined to India by dry land, and you could have walked there. India was cooler and drier, with more grassland, and animals like ostriches lived here — we know because their eggshells turn up at many sites.
They were expert hunter-gatherers: deer, antelope, wild cattle, wild pigs, birds, fish and shellfish — plus fruit, nuts, roots and honey. Ostrich eggs made a giant meal (one egg equals about two dozen hen's eggs!), and the empty shells could carry water.
Beads and patterns probably said something: "I belong to this group," "I am grown-up now," or "this was a gift from my friend." Beads could be traded between bands that lived far apart. Making art and jewellery is a sign of minds that think in symbols — the same kind of thinking that makes language, stories and writing possible.

ACT FOUR · THE ROCK WALL

Paint Like the First Artists

At Bhimbetka, the earliest paintings were made with colours from the earth — red and yellow ochre (crushed coloured rock) and white from soft white stone, mixed with water. Brushes may have been chewed twigs, or just fingers! Many of the oldest pictures show big animals, and some are huge.

ACT FIVE · HISTORY DETECTIVES

How Do We Know?

🚂 Clue 1: Seen from a train (1957)

Archaeologist V. S. Wakankar was travelling by train through Madhya Pradesh when he noticed unusual rock formations near the tracks. He went back to explore — and found the painted shelters of Bhimbetka. Today it's a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Always look out of the window!

💀 Clue 2: Bones in Sri Lankan caves

India's warm, wet soil usually destroys bones. But in Sri Lanka's caves, like Fa-Hien Lena and Batadomba-lena, bones and skeletons of modern humans survived from tens of thousands of years ago — among the oldest in South Asia. They prove people like us were here.

🧬 Clue 3: The library inside you

Every cell in your body carries DNA — a kind of instruction book passed from parents to children. By comparing the DNA of people living today, scientists can trace family lines back tens of thousands of years — to the first modern humans who reached India. In a way, you carry this history inside you!
ACT SIX · THINK ABOUT IT

Leave Your Mark

The bead-makers and painters left behind things that told the world "we were here, and this is who we are." If you could leave one thing for people 25,000 years from now, what would you choose?

🧵 What these people gave my thread: art, jewellery and symbols — the idea that a mark or an object can mean something. The red-ochre paintings of Bhimbetka are the great-great-grandparents of every rangoli, kolam, temple mural and Warli painting in India. And the beads? Indians have been making and wearing beads ever since — from Harappan carnelian to the necklace someone in your family is wearing today.
