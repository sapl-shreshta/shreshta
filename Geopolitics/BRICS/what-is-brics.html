⏱️ BRICS in one minute 🌱 BASIC

BRICS is a group (a "club") of large developing and emerging countries that meet every year to talk, cooperate and speak up together on world issues.

The name is made from the first letters of the five original members:

🏫 Think of it like this

Imagine a school where a few older students (the rich countries) have always made the rules for the playground. Some other students who have grown bigger and stronger say, "We also want a say in the rules!" So they form their own group to discuss ideas and speak together. That's a bit like BRICS.

💡 Did you know?

An "emerging economy" is a country whose economy is growing fast and becoming more modern, but where many people are still not rich. India is a great example.

📜 How BRICS was born 🌱 BASIC

BRICS started as an idea in a bank report, and only later became a real group of governments.

2001

Jim O'Neill, an economist at the bank Goldman Sachs, wrote a report. He said Brazil, Russia, India and China would become some of the world's biggest economies. He called them the "BRICs" — it sounds like "bricks", the building blocks of the future world economy! 🧱

2006

The foreign ministers of the four countries met for the first time, on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York. This is why 2026 = 20 years of BRICS. 🎂

2009

The first leaders' summit was held in Yekaterinburg, Russia.

2010

South Africa was invited to join. BRIC became BRICS (South Africa attended its first summit in 2011).

2014

BRICS agreed to create its own bank — the New Development Bank — and a safety fund called the Contingent Reserve Arrangement (you'll learn about both in Chapter 3).

2024

Big expansion! Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the UAE joined. Saudi Arabia was also invited. A new "partner country" level was created.

2025

Indonesia joined as a full member.

2026

India hosted the 18th summit in New Delhi — its 4th time as chair.

Why is it strange that a group of countries got its name from a bank report?

Usually countries choose to form a group first and then give it a name. BRICS was the other way round: an outsider (a banker making predictions for investors) invented the label, and the countries later decided they actually liked the idea of working together. It shows how ideas can shape politics.

🚀 ADVANCED: The Saudi Arabia puzzle

Saudi Arabia was invited to join in 2024. India's government lists it as a member, but Saudi Arabia has never clearly confirmed that it has fully joined. At the 2026 summit it sent its Foreign Minister, Prince Faisal bin Farhan, and some reports described it as attending as an invited guest rather than a full member.

Why might a country stay "in-between"? Saudi Arabia has close ties with the United States and also big trade with China. Staying slightly vague lets it keep good relations with both sides. In geopolitics, being deliberately unclear is sometimes a clever strategy!

💡 Memory trick for the new members

"E-E-I-U-I-S" → Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE (2024) + Indonesia (2025) + Saudi (invited).
Notice: 3 are from West Asia / the Gulf (oil!), 2 from Africa, 1 from Southeast Asia.

🤝 Partner countries 🚀 ADVANCED

Many countries wanted to join BRICS. Instead of making all of them full members, BRICS created a second level in 2024 called "partner countries". Partners are invited to some meetings but are not full members.

🌏 Asia

Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam

🌍 Africa

Nigeria, Uganda

🌎 Latin America

Bolivia, Cuba

🌍 Europe

Belarus

That's 10 partner countries. All 10 attended the 2026 summit.

Why create a "partner" level instead of just letting everyone join?
  • More members = harder to agree. BRICS decides by consensus (everyone must agree). With 30 members, agreeing on anything would be very hard.
  • It lets the club "test" new friendships before making them permanent.
  • It keeps the original members' influence strong.

It's like a sports team having main players and reserve players.

🚀 ADVANCED: What is GDP, and why do people get different numbers?

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) = the total value of all goods and services a country produces in a year. It's like the country's "total earnings".

There are two popular ways to measure it:

  • Nominal GDP — convert everything to US dollars at today's exchange rate.
  • GDP by PPP (Purchasing Power Parity) — adjust for the fact that things are cheaper in some countries. A haircut might cost ₹150 in Hyderabad but $30 (about ₹2,500) in New York. PPP says: "the haircut is the same haircut!"

Because goods are cheaper in countries like India, BRICS looks bigger when measured by PPP than by nominal GDP. That's why you'll see different percentages in different articles. A smart reader always asks: "Which measure are they using?" 🧐

🧠 PRO: One giant in the room

Among the original five, China's economy is bigger than the other four put together (in nominal dollars). Some members worry BRICS could become "China's club". India in particular wants BRICS to stay a group of equals. Keep this in mind — it explains a lot of India's behaviour in BRICS!

⚙️ How BRICS works 🚀 ADVANCED

🔄

Rotating chair

Each year a different member is the "chair" and hosts the summit. The order follows the letters: B → R → I → C → S. Russia 2024 → Brazil 2025 → India 2026 → China 2027 → South Africa 2028.

🤝

Consensus

There's no voting. A statement is only published if every member agrees. One "no" can block a sentence!

🧗

Sherpas

Each leader has a "sherpa" — a senior diplomat who negotiates the text months before the summit, just like mountain sherpas guide climbers to the top.

🗓️

A year of meetings

Ministers of finance, health, energy, education and more meet during the year. The leaders' summit is the grand finale.

If one country can block a sentence, what happens to the language when members disagree?

The words become softer and vaguer. Instead of "Country X attacked Country Y", the statement says "we are deeply concerned about rising tensions". Diplomats call this "lowest common denominator" language — the only words everyone can accept. You'll see lots of this in Chapter 5!

🚫 What BRICS is NOT 🌱 BASIC

Lots of people misunderstand BRICS. Here are the facts:

❌ Myth: BRICS is like the European Union.

✅ Fact: The EU has a parliament, laws, a common currency (the euro) and open borders. BRICS has none of these. It doesn't even have a founding treaty, a permanent headquarters office (secretariat) or its own budget.

❌ Myth: BRICS is a military alliance like NATO.

✅ Fact: BRICS members do not promise to defend each other. India and China even have a border dispute!

❌ Myth: BRICS has its own currency.

✅ Fact: There is no BRICS currency. Members are working on paying each other in their own currencies (Chapter 3).

❌ Myth: BRICS is "anti-West".

✅ Fact: Some members (Russia, Iran) do see it as a counterweight to the West. Others — like India, Brazil, the UAE and Indonesia — have close ties with the USA and Europe. PM Modi said in 2026 that BRICS is "not against anyone."

⚖️ Why join? What's hard? 🧠 PRO

👍 Why countries want to join

  • A louder voice in world institutions
  • Access to loans from the New Development Bank
  • New trade partners and markets
  • Less dependence on any one big power
  • Prestige — being "in the club"

👎 What makes BRICS hard

  • Members are very different — democracies and non-democracies, oil sellers and oil buyers
  • Rivalries inside: India–China border tension; Iran vs Gulf states
  • China's size can overshadow others
  • No treaty or secretariat → promises can be slow to happen
🧠 Big-think question

Is it better for a club to have 5 members who agree a lot, or 11 members who represent more of the world but agree less? Write down one argument for each side. (There's no single right answer — that's what makes it a great debate!)
