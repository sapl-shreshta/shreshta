2. Characteristics of Major Oceans

Generally, we speak of the oceans as if they are separate bodies of water. In reality they are just sections of one great sea covering most of the Earth's surface. The major oceans of the world are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic and Southern Oceans.

🌊 Pacific Ocean

  • The largest and deepest ocean, covering one-third of the globe; average depth 4200 m.
  • Its basin has high and abrupt ridges, deep trenches (e.g. the Mariana Trench), and a string of volcanoes along its coastal margins.
  • The equator divides it into the North Pacific Ocean and the South Pacific Ocean.
  • Pacific is a Spanish word meaning "peaceful sea" — named by the Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan.

🌊 Atlantic Ocean

  • The second largest ocean; shallower than the Pacific, with many ridges, canyons and gorges.
  • Many rivers drain into it: the Rhine, Senegal, Niger, Congo, Amazon, Mississippi, Orinoco and La Plata.
  • It is the great commercial highway of the world — most of the world's great ports lie on its coasts.
  • Occupies an elongated S-shaped basin; its name comes from Greek mythology, meaning "Sea of Atlas".
  • It is the saltiest major ocean.

🌊 Indian Ocean

  • Comparatively small in size, average depth 4000 m.
  • The two great bays on either side of the Indian peninsula — the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea — belong to it.
  • Has thousands of small islands, like the Maldives and the Cocos Islands; the Mauritius Islands are volcanic islands.
  • Principal rivers draining into it: Zambezi, Indus, Ganga and Irrawaddy.
  • It is the saltwater body covering about one-fifth of the total ocean area of the world — the smallest, geologically youngest and physically most complex of the world's three major oceans. Its deepest point is the Sunda Deep of the Java Trench (7450 m).

🌊 Arctic Ocean

  • The smallest of all oceans; situated around the North Pole and remains perpetually frozen.
  • Connected to the Atlantic Ocean by the eastern and western Greenland channels, and to the Pacific Ocean by the Bering Strait.
  • Salinity is very low; important for polar air routes between Europe and North America.
  • It is not navigable; surrounded by the important continents of Europe, North America and Asia.
  • Its surface temperature and salinity vary seasonally as the ice cover melts and freezes.

🌊 Southern (Antarctic) Ocean

  • Surrounds the continent of Antarctica; an extension of the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Oceans.
  • Remains frozen for most of the year, hence not navigable.
  • Also known as the Antarctic Ocean, it comprises the southernmost waters of the world oceans, generally taken to be south of 60°S latitude, encircling Antarctica. It is larger than the Arctic Ocean.
  • Captain James Cook, through his 1770s voyages, proved that waters encompassed the southern latitudes of the globe.
  • Its salinity is the lowest on average of the five major oceans, due to low evaporation and heavy freshwater inflow from rivers and streams. Geographers still disagree on its northern boundary, or even its existence, considering it an extension of the other three oceans.

3. Importance of Oceans

The oceans of the earth serve many functions, especially affecting the weather and temperature. They moderate the Earth's temperature by absorbing incoming solar radiation (stored as heat energy). The ever-moving ocean currents distribute this heat around the globe, heating the land and air during winter and cooling it during summer.

(a) Source of Water: An ocean is a major source of water through the water cycle — oceans contribute water vapour to the atmosphere, returned to us as precipitation.
(b) Climate: Oceans have direct control over climate. Nearness to oceans affects temperature, rain, humidity and other elements of climate.
(c) Food Resources: Oceans are the biggest storehouse of edible marine food — fish being the most important. Millions of people take fish as their food.
(d) Mineral & Chemical Resources: Oceans hold enormous mineral and chemical wealth — dissolved salts like sodium chloride (common salt), magnesium chloride, potassium chloride, etc.
(e) Oceans and Oil: Oil and gas are important fuels obtained from oceans; a large part of the world's oil production comes from off-shore fields — e.g. Mumbai High, an important off-shore oil field of India.
(f) Oceans and Energy: Oceans are becoming a source of energy too — some developed countries like France, UK and USA generate energy from tides.
(g) Trade and Transport: Oceans provide the cheapest mode of transportation for international trade — bulky products like coal, iron, machinery and transport equipment. Ocean routes act as highways for trade; despite obstacles like fog, icebergs, coral reefs and storms, oceans remain highways of trade and travel.

🐙 Did You Know? — Deep South Atlantic Discoveries

A 35-day expedition near the South Sandwich Islands uncovered rare coral gardens and possibly new species — including sponges, sea stars, and snailfish eggs laid on black coral. These zones, located 700 m deep, reveal thriving ecosystems fuelled by geochemical, not sunlight-driven, processes.

🌟 Interesting Facts

The Indian Ocean has some of the world's major ports belonging to different continents: Colombo in Sri Lanka, Durban and Richards Bay in South Africa, Jakarta in Indonesia and Melbourne in Australia.
