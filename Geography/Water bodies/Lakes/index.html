5. Lakes — Introduction & Characteristics

A lake is a large body of water on land. It is bigger and deeper than a pond. Lakes occupy hollows of the land surface in which water accumulates. They vary tremendously in size, shape, depth and mode of formation. A lake may exist temporarily, filling up a small depression of undulating ground after heavy rains — but those which are deep and carry more water than is evaporated remain permanent, e.g. Lake Geneva in Switzerland.

  • Most of the lakes in the world are freshwater lakes fed by rivers and without outflowing streams, e.g. Lake Poyang and the Great Lakes of North America.
  • In regions of low precipitation and intense evaporation, where there are few rivers strong enough to reach the sea, streams drain into a lake forming a basin of inland drainage. Due to intense evaporation, these lakes are saline — e.g. the Dead Sea has a salinity of 250 parts per thousand and the Great Salt Lake of Utah, USA, has a salinity of 220 parts per thousand.
  • Salt lakes or playas are also formed in deserts, e.g. salt lakes of Western Australia.

Importance of Lakes

In countries where lakes are found in abundance — Finland, Sweden, Canada, USA and East African countries — lakes together with other inland waterways play a dominant role in the human, economic, social and cultural life of the people.

Transport: Large lakes like the Great Lakes of North America provide a cheap and convenient form of transport for bulky, heavy goods such as coal, iron, machinery and timber.
Economic & industrial development: Early settlements and town sites were much influenced by the presence of lakes — the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence waterways developed the industries in the area.
Water storage: Natural or artificial lakes are vital sources of domestic water supply, e.g. the Okhla reservoir supplies water to Delhi.
Hydroelectric power: In mountainous districts, lakes or man-made reservoirs are used to generate hydroelectric power.
Agricultural purposes: Most freshwater lakes, once dried up, are covered with thick layers of fertile alluvium.
Modern multipurpose dams: Besides generating electric power, they also supply water for irrigation.
Source of food: Many large lakes, like the Caspian Sea and Great Lakes, have important supplies of food in the form of freshwater fish.
Source of minerals: Salt lakes provide valuable rock salts. In the Dead Sea, the highly saline water is evaporated and common salt is produced.
Control the river flow: Lakes help to control the flow of water in a river.
Tourist attractions & health resorts: Some of the world's best holiday and health resorts are on lakesides, e.g. Lake Geneva and Lake Lucerne in Switzerland — the glacier-formed lakes of the Alps made the tourist industry a national occupation for the Swiss. Chilika Lake in Odisha, India is famous for its flamingos and other migratory birds.
