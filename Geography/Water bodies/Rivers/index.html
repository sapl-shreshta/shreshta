6. Rivers — Introduction

A river is a body of water that flows downhill in a channel, usually towards the ocean. When rain falls, part of it seeps into the ground, some is evaporated back into the atmosphere, and the rest runs off as rivulets, streams and tributaries of rivers that flow down to the sea.

Key Terms

Source / HeadwatersThe beginning of a river, often in the mountains. May be fed by snowmelt, glacial melt or run-off rain — a spring, a lake, or an upland region of heavy precipitation.
Catchment areaThe uplands that collect water form the catchment areas of rivers.
Divide / WatershedThe crest of the mountain — from which streams flow down the slope to begin their journey to the ocean. A watershed is the total area of land that drains into a particular waterbody (stream, wetland, river, lake or ocean).
MouthWhere the river ends its journey — in a sea, lake or large waterbody.
DeltaIf sediments are deposited at the last stage of the river, a triangular raised area is formed; the river splits into distributaries, e.g. the Sundarban Delta and the Mississippi Delta.
EstuaryWhere the rock is hard, the river flows swiftly without carrying silt and flows straight into the sea, forming good natural harbours, e.g. in India, the Narmada and Tapi flow into the Arabian Sea forming estuaries.

Hence, every river is part of a larger system — a watershed which is the land drained by a river and its tributaries. Land uses and runoff in a watershed determine what happens in the smaller streams and waterways within it.

Characteristics of Rivers

Every river is different. However, common characteristics of rivers are as follows: the river source (headwaters) is often located in mountains, and may be fed by snowmelt, glacial melt or run-off rain.

❄️ Snow-fed Rivers

Rivers that start in high mountains from melting ice and snow. They are usually perennial in nature, e.g. rivers in Norway that flow from snow-capped mountains.

💧 Perennial Rivers

Rivers that exhibit a continuous flow of water throughout the year, except during extreme drought. Also known as permanent rivers. Very important — support activities dependent on the river throughout the year (human, plant and animal life); used for irrigation and generation of hydroelectricity, e.g. the Himalayan rivers Ganga and Yamuna.

☀️ Non-perennial (Periodic) Rivers

Rivers that do not have a constant flow throughout the year — mainly rivers which flow only during the rainy season. Found in arid climates where evaporation tends to be greater than precipitation. Stream flow declines and they may dry up, sometimes unable to reach the sea, e.g. the peninsular rivers of India — Krishna and Kaveri.

Importance of Rivers

Rivers play a very important role in the life of human beings. In the past, many rivers were cradles of great civilisation, e.g. the Indus Valley Civilisation.
Rivers serve as inland waterways for trade and travel.
River estuaries support many types of fish and birds; they are also of recreational importance for tourists. Ports can be built on them as they form good natural harbours.
River deltas support agriculture and wildlife of sea and land, and the growth of mangrove vegetation, e.g. the Sundarban Delta.
Rivers provide fresh drinking water, playing an important role in the water cycle as drainage channels for surface water.
Rivers are major economic assets of any country — they aid the production of electricity, attract tourism, act as a transport system, and help in trading purposes.
Various rivers are regarded as deities in many countries, including India — considered sacred and worshipped by devotees with utmost respect, e.g. the Ganga in India.
