When shadows fall across space

An eclipse is just a shadow — but cast by one whole world onto another. Drag the moon around its orbit and watch both kinds of eclipse form.

Move the moon around its orbit

The sun, Earth and moon only cause an eclipse when they line up almost exactly. Drag the first slider to move the moon around its monthly orbit. The moon's orbit is also tilted slightly, so try the second slider too — it jumps between different months, and in most of them the moon passes just above or below the line, missing the eclipse completely.

A CLOSER LOOK

Three ways a solar eclipse can look

Exactly how much of the sun the moon covers — and from where on Earth you're watching — changes what a solar eclipse looks like.

In a total solar eclipse, the moon's umbra reaches Earth and completely covers the sun's disc. Only the corona — the sun's faint outer atmosphere — is left glowing around the edge. This is what creates the famous "diamond ring" moment.

Three things to remember

🌕

Lunar eclipse = Earth's shadow on the moon

It happens on a full moon night, when Earth sits directly between the sun and the moon.

🌑

Solar eclipse = the moon's shadow on Earth

It happens on a new moon day, when the moon sits directly between the sun and Earth.

💍

Every eclipse is really just a shadow

Total, partial, and annular are simply different ways of standing in the umbra, penumbra, or the tip of a shadow that doesn't quite reach.
